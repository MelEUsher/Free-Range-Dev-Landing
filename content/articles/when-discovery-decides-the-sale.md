Discovery is the most-written-about subject in sales, and most of that writing treats it as a warm-up. Build rapport, ask a few open-ended questions, find the pain, then move into the presentation. It becomes something to get through before the real work starts.

That description doesn't survive an appointment where the outcome rests on it. I sold commission-only in three industries, in settings that had nothing in common with each other. What held everywhere was that by the time I opened anything and started presenting, the outcome was mostly already set, and it was set by the questions I'd asked in the first ten minutes.

That's a harder claim than it sounds, because it means the pressure sits in the part nobody rehearses. Most people practice the presentation, which is the part that varies least. Anyone can learn to say the same twelve things in the same order. The judgment is in deciding which of those twelve things this particular person needs to hear, and that comes from what you ask before you say anything at all.

## How the Presentation Gets Assembled

Here's the practical version of that claim. If your presentation comes out the same every time, the answers you collected never changed anything.

I carried everything I could present into every appointment. What changed from one appointment to the next was which pieces I brought out, in what order, and what I left in the bag entirely. Discovery is how you decide that. Often it tells you which half of your material is now irrelevant, and cutting that half is most of the value you deliver in the next hour. Sometimes it tells you that the thing a person actually wants is something they didn't know to ask for.

A man came into my office for homeowner's insurance. Before we finished, he asked whether I thought he needed life insurance, because he wanted his sister to have a way to pay for his funeral. He had a figure in mind already, ten thousand dollars, and I could have written that policy in a few minutes.

I kept asking instead. He was older and single, with no children. His house and his car were paid for, so what he was carrying was the thought of leaving his sister to settle an estate and arrange a funeral on her own. Then he told me how he spent his time. Most of it went to a church-run thrift store that funded the local homeless shelter and food pantry, where he sorted and priced and set out donations. He gave to both agencies every month.

That answer changed what I pulled out of my bag. A ten-thousand-dollar whole life policy pays for a funeral. It reaches nothing else in the life he'd just spent twenty minutes describing. So I showed him what a larger universal life policy could do, and he did the rest of the thinking himself. He bought a policy for a hundred and fifty thousand dollars and left his sister instructions: cover the final expenses, then divide what remained four ways, among herself, the church, the shelter, and the food pantry.

The questions had changed too. Once I understood what he was protecting, they moved off coverage amounts and onto who he wanted taken care of, and in what order.

He left excited about the four places the money would go.

## A Question Can Close the Room

The part that almost never makes it into discovery advice is that questions carry risk. Writers treat asking as free, as though the worst outcome is a short answer. A wrong question in the first few minutes ends the appointment. They won't tell you it ended. They'll get polite, they'll get brief, and you'll present to a person who has already decided the answer is no.

A few kinds of questions do that on their own. Anything that asks a person to justify a decision they already made puts them on the defensive. Anything that reveals you're aiming at a product rather than at them tells them you're running a script. Anything that asks for a level of disclosure the conversation hasn't earned yet, usually about money, reads as presumptuous even when the information is relevant. Once trust drops, more questions won't bring it back. You finish the appointment on whatever is left.

There's a worse version of this, and it has nothing to do with trust.

Early on, at Job Corps, I stood in front of about thirty students and the parents and foster parents who'd come with them. It was the one sales job I didn't do on commission. I was describing the different centers, and I asked the room how they'd like to travel to Kentucky and live there for six months while learning a trade.

I hadn't fully prepared for that particular group. Most of those students were sixteen and in the foster system, and they couldn't leave the state until they turned eighteen. The adults in the room caught it before the kids did, and several of them were angry.

With a room of mostly sixteen-year-olds, the adults decide. A night like that usually ended with about ten applications. That one ended with two. I had opened by offering those students something they weren't allowed to take.

Nothing I asked afterward recovered it. The question didn't put anyone on the defensive, which is the failure everyone warns you about. It told the room I didn't know who I was talking to, and that one is harder to undo, because no question fixes it. I should have found out who was going to be in that room before I walked in.

## Ask About the Situation

Questions about a person's situation work better than questions about the purchase.

Questions about the purchase produce sales answers. If you ask what someone is looking for, or what their budget is, or what would make them comfortable moving forward, you get the answer they think a salesperson wants, and it's usually vague enough to be useless. Questions about their actual situation produce real answers, because the person is now describing their life rather than negotiating with you. Most people can describe their week accurately, because they lived it. A question about the purchase asks them to predict what will satisfy them, which is a guess even when they answer honestly.

The information you need is in the second kind of answer: what they do on a normal day, what they've already tried, who else is affected, what changed recently to make this worth a call. None of that sounds like sales, and all of it tells you what the decision will turn on.

In real estate, the questions I leaned on were about family: "Who lives with you?" "Who visits, and how often?" "How old are the children, or the grandchildren?" It sounds like small talk, and the answers decided which houses I showed and which rooms I lingered in.

Buyers arrive with a list: a big backyard, a large kitchen. The list is real, but it's a guess at what will make their life feel the way they want it to. It's usually built out of the house they're living in now. A retired couple whose grandchildren are past the age of playing outside may not actually want a big backyard. They may prefer a spectacular view they don't have to maintain instead. That same couple asking for a large kitchen may instead want a beautiful and spatially functional one they can cook for four in, rather than one that takes an afternoon to clean.

That isn't something the list will tell you. It comes out when they describe their week: who's in the house, who comes to visit, and what everybody does when those people are over. Then you show them the house that fits their life, including the features they didn't know to ask for.

Here's what the presentation sounds like once you have that. In a house with a smaller yard: "The backyard on this property is a little smaller than the other one you looked at online, but the view from the sitting room is spectacular. The wonderful part about that is you have less to mow and still get to enjoy that landscape out your window." In the kitchen: "This kitchen is smaller than what you described, but as you can see, it's full of luxury amenities that make it more functional, with less space to clean when you've finished dinner."

Both of those name the gap between what they asked for and what they're standing in, and then hand it back to them in the terms of their own life. Neither one is available to you if you haven't asked about their week.

## Listen for What They Volunteer

The most useful information often arrives when you haven't asked for it.

When someone adds a detail you didn't request, or circles back to something twenty minutes later, or tells you about a situation from three years ago that isn't obviously related, that's the real reason surfacing. People rarely lead with the actual driver of a decision. They lead with the acceptable version of it, and the driver comes out sideways once they're comfortable. If you're busy running your next question, you'll miss it, because it rarely sounds important when it lands.

Go back to the man in my office. Nothing about a thrift store belongs in a conversation about a funeral policy. He mentioned it because I'd asked how he spent his time and he was comfortable enough to answer at length. He never offered it as a reason to buy anything. It was the reason, and he hadn't connected it to insurance yet. If I'd had my mind on my next question about beneficiaries, I'd have written the ten-thousand-dollar policy, and both of us would have called it a good appointment.

The habit here is a small one. When a person volunteers something, stop and follow it instead of returning to your own thread. You planned that thread with less information than you have now.

## Know When You Are Finished Asking

Discovery ends when you can say what this person needs and why, in a sentence, and be confident they'd agree with it. Until you can do that, you're not ready to present, no matter how long you've been sitting there. If you present anyway, you're guessing, and people can tell.

Sometimes the person tells you. In an easy appointment, the buyer starts naming what they like about the property, finding the selling features on their own before you've pointed at a single one. When someone begins selling the house back to you, you can start moving toward the presentation.

The harder appointments come down to a question you ask yourself. Do you have enough about this person to present to them as a warm close? If the answer is no, there are two reasons for it. Either you have more discovery to do, or this person isn't a buyer for what you're selling.

It's also why the lines about the yard and the kitchen work as a handoff. You can't say either one without already knowing how the person lives, so the sentence that starts the presentation is built out of the answers you just collected.

There's a step between more discovery and deciding someone isn't a buyer. In insurance, I'd sometimes reach the end of every question I could think to ask and still get a no when I asked about life coverage. What I'd do then was walk the person through two versions of the same event (their passing with a policy in place, and their passing without one) and what each version leaves the family holding. That gives someone something real to weigh, and the answer they give afterward is the one that counts.

Pushing into a presentation while a person is still sitting in that no costs you the sale and potentially any sale with them later. From their side, it's pressure from someone who wasn't listening.

The short version is this: ask before you assemble, know that one question can cost you the room, ask about the situation rather than the purchase, follow what people volunteer, and hold the presentation until you can state a person's need back to them and have them agree.
